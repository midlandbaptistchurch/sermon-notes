## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues his series on the King James Bible by focusing on John Wycliffe (c. 1328–1384), known as the "Morning Star of the Reformation," and his foundational role in producing the first English Bible translation. The lesson establishes Wycliffe's intellectual formation at Oxford, the theological influence of Archbishop Thomas Bradwardine's defense of Augustinian grace against Pelagianism, and Wycliffe's growing opposition to Roman Catholic doctrine and clerical corruption. Taylor frames Wycliffe's life and work as an early but essential stage in the seven-step purification process he sees reflected in Psalm 12:6-7, ultimately leading to the King James Bible.

## Scripture
Psalm 12:6-7 — Cited as the biblical foundation for the doctrine of divine preservation of God's Word: "The words of the LORD are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. Thou shalt keep them, O LORD, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever."

## Notes

### Review and Context
- Series has covered the backdrop and development of the English language as context for understanding the King James Bible issue.
- English is a growing global language, making the KJV especially significant for worldwide Bible distribution.
- Psalm 12:6-7 is applied to the iterative, multi-stage translation process that produced the KJV — Taylor identifies seven "purification" stages to be covered across the series.

### John Wycliffe — Who Was He?
- Born c. 1328–1330 in Yorkshire, England, into a wealthy family.
- Entered Oxford University around age 15, when it was already one of Europe's greatest universities.
- Historian Montague Burrows (1884, *Wycliffe's Place in History*) wrote: "To Wycliffe we owe more than to any one person — our English language, our English Bible, and our Reformed religion."
- Dr. David Allen is quoted asserting that without Wycliffe there would have been no William Tyndale, and the course of British, European, and world history would have been different.
- Became known as the **Morning Star of the Reformation**.

### The Black Death and Thomas Bradwardine
- The Black Death killed approximately one-third of England's population during Wycliffe's years at Oxford.
- Among those who died was **Thomas Bradwardine**, Archbishop of Canterbury (d. 26 August 1349), a defender of grace and free will against the Pelagians.
- Bradwardine's book *On the Cause of God* argued strongly for the Pauline-Augustinian doctrine of grace: "The cause of God is the cause of grace and the cause of grace is the cause of God."
- **Pelagius** (British theologian) had taught a heretical view denying the necessity of grace for salvation.
- **Augustine of Hippo** (354–430) opposed Pelagianism; his position — that humanity cannot attain righteousness through its own effort due to original sin — became the Pauline-Augustinian Doctrine of Grace.
- Bradwardine's time at Oxford overlapped with Wycliffe's by approximately three to four years, and his teachings significantly shaped Wycliffe's theology.

### Wycliffe's Academic Career
- Completed his doctoral requirements at Oxford by delivering a lecture series on the **entire Bible** — his doctoral thesis.
- Kept on as a teacher at Oxford; quickly became its **leading philosopher and theologian**.
- Wrote extensively in Latin, producing treatises opposing the spiritual sterility and corruption of the institutional church.
- Believed strongly in the **primacy of preaching**: over 360 of his sermons survive today.
- Notable sermon excerpt on the Passion of Christ, calling believers to submit all five senses to Christ in obedience.
- Student **Geoffrey Chaucer** studied under Wycliffe at Canterbury Hall, Oxford (c. 1367); Wycliffe's influence is reflected in *Canterbury Tales*, which critiques clerical corruption and models the character of "the poor parson" on Wycliffe himself.

### Wycliffe's Conflict with the Church
- Wycliffe attacked abuses of church power from priest to pope.
- Insisted Christ gave the church authority over the **spiritual** realm, not the temporal.
- In **1381** he was banished from Oxford — a place he called his greatest love.
- After his removal from Oxford, he moved beyond criticizing church abuses and began attacking **Roman Catholic doctrine** directly.
- Argued that the Pope's authority could not be placed on par with Scripture.
- Preached the same message of God's grace in Christ that would resound again in the Reformation 200 years later — making him a man "ahead of his time."
