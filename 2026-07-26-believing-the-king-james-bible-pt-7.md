## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues his series on believing the King James Bible by tracing the development of Middle English following the Norman Conquest of 1066, showing how French became the language of England's ruling class while Old English survived among the common people. He examines how English gradually reasserted itself in law, government, literature, and eventually the church — often over fierce opposition — and draws a theological conclusion: God providentially used the "base" language of common people to produce and preserve His Word in English.

## Scripture
1 Corinthians 1:26-29 — Paul's declaration that God chooses the foolish, weak, and base things of the world to confound the mighty; applied here to the rise of the English language over elite French. Daniel 2:20 — Daniel's praise that God removes and sets up kings and changes times and seasons; applied to God's sovereignty over the Norman Conquest and its ultimate contribution to the English language.

## Key Themes
- The Norman Conquest (1066) and its creation of a bilingual, class-divided England
- French as the language of power and Latin as the language of the church vs. English as the language of the common people
- The gradual legal and governmental recovery of English: the Statute of Pleading (1362) and the Chancery Standard (c. 1413–1422)
- The church's resistance to English: Thomas Arundel's 1407 prohibition against translating Scripture into English, and John Wycliffe's pioneering work
- Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* as a landmark of Middle English literature that legitimized the language
- God's providential sovereignty over language history, ensuring English would become the vehicle for His preserved Word

## Notes
- **Old English recap:** Originated c. 450–500 AD; ~50,000–60,000 words; about 25% of modern English vocabulary and ~60% of everyday spoken words trace back to Old English.
- **Norman Conquest (14 October 1066):** William the Conqueror defeated King Harold II (last Anglo-Saxon king) at the Battle of Hastings; conquest fully consolidated over ~4 years.
- **Linguistic stratification:** Norman French became the language of the ruling class, government, and courts; Latin was used by the clergy and in academia; English remained the language of common people. Roughly 10,000 French words entered English, of which ~7,000 survive in modern English.
- **French loanwords:** Words such as *government*, *justice*, *language*, *pork*, *beef*, *mutton*, and *poultry* all entered English from French during this period.
- **Statute of Pleading (1362):** Parliament required all court proceedings to be conducted in English (while records could still be kept in Latin), marking the beginning of modern legal English.
- **Chancery Standard (Henry V, r. 1413–1422):** A standardized written English developed by government scribal offices, which helped regularize English spelling and usage; early printer Richard Pynson further promoted this standard in the late 1490s–early 1500s.
- **Church resistance:** The institutional church was the slowest to accept English. Thomas Arundel, Archbishop of Canterbury, issued a 1407 decree forbidding any translation of Scripture into English and banning Wycliffe's existing translations — making English in the church tantamount to heresy.
- **John Wycliffe:** Identified as the pioneer who began translating the Bible into the vernacular English of his day, sparking the debate over whether English was capable of conveying deep theological truth.
- **Geoffrey Chaucer and the *Canterbury Tales* (c. 1400):** A collection of 24 tales (of a planned larger set) framed as a storytelling contest among 30 pilgrims traveling to Canterbury; written in Middle English, it greatly elevated the prestige of English literature and introduced iambic pentameter to English verse.
- **The Hundred Years' War:** Prolonged conflict between England and France helped consolidate English national identity and turned popular sentiment against the French language, ultimately leaving English as "the last language standing."
- **Professor Alastair McGrath** (*In the Beginning: The Story of the King James Bible*): Quoted several times as a scholarly King James proponent, though Taylor notes McGrath holds inconsistent views elsewhere (theistic evolution, ordination in the Church of England).
- **Application:** Taylor draws a parallel between the social contempt for English and 1 Corinthians 1:26-29 — God chose what the world considered base and despised to accomplish His purposes. The language of the common people became the language of God's preserved Word.
