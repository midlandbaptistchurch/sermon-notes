## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues the series on dealing with temptation by emphasizing God's promise to provide an escape route from every temptation we face. He illustrates this promise through practical examples and analogies, encouraging believers to plan ahead by thinking through their vulnerabilities and developing strategies to recognize and take God's way of escape when temptation arises.

## Scripture
1 Corinthians 10:13 — "God is faithful who will not suffer you to be tempted above that you're able, but will with the temptation also make a way to escape that you may be able to bear it."

## Key Themes
- God's faithfulness in providing escape routes from temptation
- The importance of planning ahead to anticipate temptation
- The critical role of pausing to think before reacting
- Temptation as a decision point where we choose between truth and compromise
- The necessity of recognizing our ongoing susceptibility to temptation at every stage of life
- Leading by example, especially for younger generations

## Notes

### Three Types of Temptation
Taylor clarifies that temptation in Scripture has multiple meanings. The primary focus is on temptation as an enticement to sin—a mental, visual, or physical pull toward wrongdoing. Every sin has a precursor of temptation; temptation represents the critical opportunity to avoid sin entirely.

### The Escape Route Promise
God promises to provide a way of escape with every temptation, analogous to emergency exit lights on an airplane. However, this escape route only helps if we choose to take it—it requires exercising our free will in a godly way.

### Decision Points Throughout Life
Taylor uses personal examples and family stories to illustrate how life consists of repeated decision points:
- Young people deciding whether to continue church attendance after leaving home
- Breaking cycles of addiction (such as alcoholism) through conscious choice
- Standing for truth even when compromise would be easier
- Routine decisions (like attending church) that become habitual but remain choices

These decision points become increasingly important during transitions, such as when young adults move out or start making independent choices.

### Planning and Preparation
Effective dealing with temptation requires simple but meaningful planning:
- Anticipate your personal temptations based on your life stage and circumstances
- Develop a plan tailored to where you are now (temptations change with age)
- The simplest plan is often the most effective: "Stop and think. Pause."
- Even a momentary pause gives your conscience and spirit a chance to intervene before you react

Taylor acknowledges that most people don't naturally plan for things that may not happen, even though planning for temptation is essential. He contrasts this with how readily people plan for desired outcomes (like buying a truck).

### Accountability Across Generations
Taylor emphasizes that we remain accountable for how we handle temptation, particularly in view of younger generations watching us. He notes the difference between being a parent and a grandparent—grandparents often display greater patience—and expresses his desire for his grandchildren to respect (rather than idolize) him by seeing him handle temptation well.

### Practical Examples
- Alcohol: Taylor shares his family history with alcoholism and his gratitude that his son has successfully avoided it
- Impatience with children: Even a brief moment of losing patience with a grandchild can leave lasting impressions
- Age and changing temptations: What tempts us in youth differs from what tempts us in old age; our plans must evolve accordingly
