## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues the "Dealing with Temptation" series by exploring the three meanings of temptation found in 1 Corinthians 10:13: trials/tests, provocation/incitement, and enticement to sin. He emphasizes the importance of viewing our lives through the lens of the judgment seat of Christ and remaining vigilant against temptations, both obvious and subtle, that surround us constantly.

## Scripture
1 Corinthians 10:13 — Paul's promise that no temptation will overcome us beyond what we are able to bear, and that God will provide a way of escape.

James 1:13 — God cannot be tempted with evil, and He does not tempt anyone to sin, though we are drawn away by our own lusts.

## Key Themes
- Three meanings of temptation: trial/test, provocation/incitement, and enticement to sin
- The importance of rightly dividing Scripture and studying God's word consistently
- Living with the judgment seat of Christ as our motivation and reference point
- Recognizing that believers are "the salt of the earth" with influence in their culture
- The reality that temptation is personal and present in every moment
- The need for preventive prayer and spiritual vigilance rather than only corrective measures
- Understanding lust as an internal force that works in concert with external temptation

## Notes

### The Three Meanings of Temptation
Taylor breaks down 1 Corinthians 10:13 as containing "three buts," each explaining a different aspect of temptation:

1. **Trial or Test** — God tests our faith and obedience, as He did with Abraham. In our age, this test is living according to God's word despite life's challenges.

2. **Provocation or Incitement** — We can provoke God or one another. The example of the Lord's Supper demonstrates how serious God is about proper observance and attitude in worship.

3. **Enticement to Sin** — This is the most immediate and personal form of temptation, where external temptations appeal to our internal lusts and draw us away from God's path.

### Long-Term Thinking and the Judgment Seat of Christ
Taylor emphasizes that believers should live with an eternal perspective, constantly asking: "What will this look like at the judgment seat of Christ?" He compares preventive spiritual health (Bible study, prayer, obedience) to taking vitamins—the benefits may not be immediately visible, but their absence has serious long-term consequences.

### The Personal Nature of Temptation
Taylor stresses that temptation is intimate and constant—we are "one temptation away" from potential failure. He warns against becoming academic or detached when studying temptation, insisting that believers must recognize and resist the specific temptations they personally face. He acknowledges his own struggles and fears, modeling transparency about ongoing spiritual battles.

### The Internal and External Battle
Temptation works by appealing to our internal lusts (which take many forms: sexual desire, lust for power, money, fame, and pride) through external stimuli. Believers must be alert and exercise their spiritual senses constantly.

### Preventive versus Corrective Prayer
Taylor notes that many believers live reactively, only praying when problems arise. Instead, he advocates for preventive prayer and decision-making—pausing to seek God's guidance before entering situations, rather than scrambling for help after mistakes are made.

### Being Salt and Light in Culture
Taylor emphasizes that Christians have real influence in their society as "the salt of the earth." He references the strategic importance of American values and leadership in the world, and warns that without Christian influence, culture deteriorates into chaos. He also makes a prophetic observation about New York City's financial trajectory.
