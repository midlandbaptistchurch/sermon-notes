## Summary
Pat Boulton delivers a conversational Sunday evening message urging the congregation to think seriously about God, eternity, and the souls of those around them. Drawing on the Parable of the Rich Fool in Luke 12, the story of Naaman in 2 Kings 5, and Felix's delayed response in Acts 24, he challenges believers not to postpone either their own walk with God or their witness to the lost. He closes with a practical call for church members to seek training in soul-winning so they are equipped to share the Gospel at any opportunity.

## Scripture
Luke 12:16-21 — The Parable of the Rich Fool, in which a wealthy man plans for earthly prosperity without thought for God or eternity, only to have his soul required of him that very night.

Also referenced:
- Psalm 143:8-9 — A morning prayer for God's guidance and loving-kindness.
- 2 Kings 5:1-14 — Naaman the leper's healing through humble obedience to Elisha's instruction.
- Acts 24:25 — Felix trembles under Paul's preaching but defers his decision to a "convenient season."
- James 4:14 — Life described as a vapor.

## Key Themes
- The danger of not thinking about God or eternity
- The urgency of witnessing — there is never a "convenient" time
- The brevity of life (James 4:14) as motivation for action
- Overcoming pride as a barrier to salvation (Naaman) and to sharing the Gospel
- The importance of discipleship and practical soul-winning training
- God's providence in connecting believers with the lost across language and cultural barriers

## Notes
- Boulton opens with Psalm 143:8-9 as a recommended morning devotional verse, framing the sermon around the idea of being directed by God each day.
- The central text is Luke 12:16-21 (the Rich Fool). Boulton identifies the man's core failure as not thinking about God — he planned for material abundance but gave no thought to his soul or to eternity.
- He parallels this with the story of Naaman (2 Kings 5), who nearly missed his healing through pride. Just as Naaman resisted simple obedience, unsaved people often resist the simplicity of the Gospel.
- Acts 24:25 is used to illustrate the fatal habit of delay: Felix trembled but sent Paul away, promising to call at a "convenient season." Boulton notes that for many people that convenient season never comes, referencing a man in the congregation's circle who died by suicide shortly before the service.
- Boulton shares a personal account of connecting a Ukrainian motel worker named Marina with Eugene, a Ukrainian missionary now living in Pennsylvania, demonstrating creative and cross-cultural soul-winning.
- He shares a touching account of leading an elderly Polish nursing home resident, Genevieve Klemyszewski, to faith, which later opened a door to minister to her son-in-law.
- He illustrates the need for discipleship using a story told by his son-in-law Adam: as a teenager Adam was handed a chainsaw and sent onto a fallen tree in a flooded river — he had no instruction on what to do when the tree broke. New Christians, Boulton argues, are often in the same position: saved but untrained.
- Practical challenge: 168 hours in a week. After work, sleep, eating, and entertainment, the average person has roughly three hours for God. Boulton calls the congregation to reclaim time and to seek soul-winning training from people in the church such as Riley.
- Closing prayer asks God to make Midland Baptist a "soul-winning station" where members share the Gospel every week.
