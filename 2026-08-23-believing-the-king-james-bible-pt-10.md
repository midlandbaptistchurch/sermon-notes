## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues his Sunday School series on the King James Bible by completing the study of the English language's development and addressing common misconceptions about why the KJV is sometimes mistakenly called "Old English." He then introduces early Bible translations in various languages before turning attention to John Wycliffe, the "Morning Star of the Reformation," as the starting point for the history of English Bible translations.

## Scripture
Psalm 12:6-7 — Used to illustrate God's promise to preserve His pure words, applied to the iterative translation process that produced the King James Bible.

## Key Themes
- Common misconceptions about the King James Bible being written in "Old English"
- The significance of "thee/thou" vs. "ye/you" as singular and plural second-person pronouns
- Satan's ongoing effort to undermine confidence in God's Word
- God's interactive process with man in producing and preserving Scripture
- Early Bible translations in non-English languages predating the KJV
- John Wycliffe as the Morning Star of the Reformation and pioneer of English Bible translation
- The importance of individuals having direct access to God's Word

## Notes

### Why People Mistake the KJV for "Old English"
Dr. Sam Gipp's *Understandable History of the Bible* is cited as a primary source. Three reasons are given for the misconception:
1. **Gothic typeface** — The original 1611 KJV was printed in gothic type, sometimes called "Old English" font, which looks ornate and unfamiliar. Later editions switched to Roman type.
2. **Thee/thou/ye/you** — These second-person pronouns were common speech in 1611 and are still useful today. "Thee/thou/thy" = second person singular (one person). "Ye/you" = second person plural (more than one person).
3. **Deliberate mischaracterization** — Some critics label KJV English as archaic or obsolete to steer people away from it, which Taylor identifies as a tactic rooted in Satan's broader effort to cast doubt on God's Word, echoing the Garden of Eden.

### God's Interactive Process in Producing Scripture
- God authored Scripture through human writers (e.g., Paul's secretaries penning his epistles).
- The Ten Commandments illustrate God's willingness to work with and through imperfect humans.
- Misprints and errors (e.g., a misplaced "not" in a personal Oxford KJV copy) do not invalidate the whole; God superintends the process and corrections can be identified through study.
- The Bible is not like an encyclopedia requiring annual updates — it is complete and set.

### Early Bible Translations (Pre-KJV, Non-English)
A survey of early versions demonstrates God's heart for people to have His Word in their native tongue:
- **Syriac Peshitta** — 2nd century
- **Old Latin** — c. 157 AD (used by common people; the Roman Catholic Church later commissioned Jerome's Latin Vulgate in 380 AD to replace it)
- **Coptic** (four dialects) — 2nd–4th century
- **Armenian** — Late 4th / early 5th century
- **Ethiopian** — Early centuries
- **French** (four versions), **Italian** (1471), **Spanish** (16th century), **German** (Luther's and Nuremberg), **Flemish** (1548), **Danish** (1550), **Bohemian** (1539), **Polish** (1390), **Slovenian/Ancient Russian** (1581)

Many of these predate the English language itself, let alone the KJV. None, however, fulfilled God's goal of a single language capable of reaching the entire world — a role English and the KJV would fill.

### Psalm 12:6-7 Applied to the KJV
"The words of the Lord are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. Thou shalt keep them, O Lord, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever." Taylor applies this to the iterative translation process leading to the King James Bible.

### John Wycliffe — Morning Star of the Reformation
- Wycliffe predated Martin Luther and was among the first to protest Roman Catholic corruption by appealing directly to Scripture.
- He translated the Bible into English from the Latin (not from Greek and Hebrew), motivated by a desire for common people to have God's Word directly.
- The Roman Catholic Church suppressed Scripture to maintain power and control over the people.
- Wycliffe argued — and Taylor affirms — that every believer has a direct, individual relationship with God through Jesus Christ as the sole mediator, making personal access to Scripture essential.
- Future lessons will detail Wycliffe's translation, his followers, and the persecution they faced.
