## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues his series on believing the King James Bible by exploring the historical development of the English language — from the Tower of Babel through Old English and Anglo-Saxon invasions — arguing that God providentially shaped the English language over roughly one thousand years in order to produce the King James Bible. Drawing on Genesis 11, he frames the confounding of languages at Babel as the backdrop against which God later worked through historical events, often tragic ones, to bring forth a language capable of carrying His Word to the world. The lesson closes with a preview of upcoming sessions that will examine specific events in Old English history that directly shaped the language of the KJV.

## Scripture
Genesis 11:1-9 — The account of the Tower of Babel, used as the foundational backdrop for understanding why there are many languages in the world and how God later worked through the English language's development.

## Key Themes
- God's providential ordering of history to produce the English language and the King James Bible
- The simplicity of Scripture vs. Satan's desire to complicate and confuse
- Faith as the proper posture toward God's Word
- The interactive nature of divine inspiration — God working through human authors
- The Tower of Babel as the origin of linguistic diversity
- English language history: Old English, Anglo-Saxon invasions, and linguistic development
- Unity in diversity — how scattered languages were drawn together into a prominent world tongue
- The KJV's 12,143 unique English words as a feature, not a limitation

## Notes
- Taylor recaps previous sessions: Satan seeks to complicate Scripture; God desires simplicity; the Bible is an interactive process between God and man.
- **Genesis 11 as backdrop:** The whole earth once had one language (v. 1); God confounded speech at Babel (v. 7-9) and scattered mankind — the world is still living with that reality.
- **Divine destiny of English:** Taylor draws a parallel between the American concept of "manifest destiny" and what he calls a "divine destiny" for the English language — God assembling a world-prominent tongue through which to give His Word.
- **English language statistics presented:**
  - An estimated 14.7 new English words are created every day.
  - Oxford English Dictionary contains ~273,000 headwords; Webster's Third has ~470,000 entries; total including technical/slang terms may exceed 1 million.
  - A native English speaker actively uses 20,000–35,000 words.
  - A fluent non-native speaker can operate comfortably with 8,000–12,000 words.
  - Just 1,000 words cover over 85% of everyday conversation.
  - **The King James Bible uses 12,143 unique English words.**
- **Three periods of English development:** Old English (Anglo-Saxon, ~449–1100 AD), Middle English, and Modern English. The KJV is Modern English, not Old English.
- **Old English / Anglo-Saxon origins:** When Anglo-Saxons, Saxons, and Jutes invaded Britain in 449 AD (following Roman withdrawal), their Germanic languages blended with native Celtic tongues. Old English used six vowels (including the æ ligature) and was divided into four dialects: Northumbrian, Mercian, West Saxon, and Kentish.
- **Beowulf** is cited as the most famous Old English literary work — anonymous, surviving in a single manuscript (the Nowell Codex, ~975–1025 AD) — and is essentially unreadable to modern English speakers without translation.
- **Key theological application:** Just as Joseph told his brothers "you intended it for bad, but God intended it for good" (Genesis 50:20), God used invasions and difficult historical events to forge the English language that ultimately carried His Word.
- Taylor notes he is aware the content is historically detailed and thanks the class for bearing with the academic nature of the study, emphasizing the goal is appreciation for God's work in producing the English Bible.
- **Next session** will cover key events within Old English history that specifically impacted the language of the King James Bible.
