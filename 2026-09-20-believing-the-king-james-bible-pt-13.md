## Summary
Brother Hugh Taylor continues his Sunday School series on believing the King James Bible, tracing the historical progression from John Wycliffe's Middle English Bible through the Protestant Reformation to the threshold of examining the Textus Receptus. He reflects on Psalm 12:6-7 as a foundation for God's preservation of His pure word, and identifies the key historical developments — movable type printing, affordable paper, Erasmus's Greek New Testament, and the Protestant Reformation — that set the stage for the eventual production of the King James Bible. The lesson closes with a preview of upcoming study into the Textus Receptus and its role as the source text for the KJV.

## Scripture
Psalm 12:6-7 — Used as the theological anchor for the series: "The words of the LORD are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. Thou shalt keep them, O LORD, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever." Taylor applies this passage to the iterative translation process culminating in the King James Bible.

## Key Themes
- God's preservation of His pure word through history
- The seven purifications of Scripture in the English language leading to the King James Bible
- John Wycliffe as the "Morning Star of the Reformation" and the foot-in-the-door for an English Bible
- Key historical developments between Wycliffe and Tyndale: movable type printing, affordable paper, Erasmus's 1516 Greek New Testament, and the 1517 Protestant Reformation
- The Textus Receptus (Received Text) as the source behind the KJV and related English translations
- The importance of educated, informed faith — studying to show oneself approved
- Paradigm shifts required for spiritual growth, grounded in the Word of God
- The value of physical Bible familiarity and tactile study over digital reading

## Notes
- Taylor returns from vacation and notes Brother Bill filled in the previous week.
- **Wycliffe's Bible** (1380s): Translated from Latin into Middle English — not from original Hebrew and Greek. Considered the starting point, not part of the seven purifications, because: (1) it was in Middle English, not modern English, and (2) it was not translated from the original languages.
- **Seven purifications** in English leading to the KJV are mentioned but only partially enumerated in this session; the Great Bible (1539) and Bishops' Bible (1568) are cited as examples, with the King James Bible (1611) as the culmination.
- **Key developments between Wycliffe and Tyndale (1525):**
  - Movable type printing — dramatically reduced cost and time of Bible reproduction.
  - Affordable paper — previously a taxed, precious commodity.
  - Erasmus's Greek New Testament (1516) — provided a direct Greek source bypassing the Latin.
  - The Protestant Reformation (1517) — created a cultural and religious climate favorable to Bible translation in the vernacular.
- **Erasmus** (full name: Desiderius Erasmus) produced his Greek New Testament in 1516, one year before Luther's Reformation.
- The Reformation did not directly produce the KJV but created a climate that made it possible.
- Taylor affirms there are true believers saved through the Reformation despite incomplete doctrinal understanding (e.g., of Paul's ministry).
- Discussion touches on the Roman Catholic Church's use of Latin and its historical suppression of lay Bible reading, and the lasting mindset that priests must interpret Scripture.
- Taylor reflects on the importance of carrying and reading a physical Bible versus relying on phones/tablets, noting research suggesting digital reading undermines retention.
- **Textus Receptus** is introduced as the Greek source text from which the KJV and its predecessor English translations were made — to be studied in detail beginning next week.
- Next session will examine Erasmus and other contributors to the Textus Receptus production history.
- Closing thought: You do not need to know the history to have faith in the KJV, but understanding it enhances confidence and competence in the Word.
