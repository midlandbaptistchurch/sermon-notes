## Summary
In this sixth installment of his Sunday School series on the King James Bible, Hugh Taylor traces the historical development of the English language from Old English through the beginning of Middle English, highlighting the Anglo-Saxon period, Viking invasions, the Catholic mission of Augustine to Kent, and the Norman Conquest of 1066. Throughout, Taylor draws attention to God's sovereign providence in superintending these often violent and chaotic historical events to shape the very language in which the King James Bible would eventually be written.

## Scripture
No specific scripture passage is the primary focus of this lesson. Romans 8:28 is referenced in application: "All things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."

## Key Themes
- The providential development of the English language across three periods: Old English, Middle English, and Modern English
- God's sovereignty over human history, including conquest and cultural upheaval, to preserve and develop the language of the King James Bible
- The influence of the Anglo-Saxon invasion, Viking raids, Augustine's Catholic mission, and the Norman Conquest on English vocabulary
- The distinction between Germanic/Anglo-Saxon everyday words and French/Latin formal words absorbed into English
- The resilience of the English language under pressure from invading cultures and tongues

## Notes
- **Old English (c. 449–1100 AD):** Formed through the Anglo-Saxon invasion of Britain in 449 AD, blending Germanic, Celtic, and later Norse influences. Old English had an estimated 50,000–60,000 words, used six vowels, and featured four regional dialects including Kentish.
- **King Ethelbert of Kent and Augustine (597 AD):** King Ethelbert of Kent married Bertha, a Frankish Catholic princess. Her influence (and that of her father's connections to Pope Gregory) led to Augustine being sent as a missionary from Rome, landing in East Kent in 597 AD. Ethelbert converted to Christianity, and this Catholic mission brought Latin and Greek words into English vocabulary.
- **Celtic Christianity:** Taylor notes that prior to Augustine, Celtic Christianity (non-Roman Catholic) had taken hold in Britain, with Saint Patrick as a famous example of a Celtic missionary to Ireland.
- **Viking Invasions (c. 750–1050 AD):** Norse-speaking Vikings invaded and occupied parts of England, contributing monosyllabic words such as "sky," "skin," and "root" to the English language.
- **Old English vocabulary legacy:** About 25% of the modern English vocabulary derives from Germanic/Anglo-Saxon roots, yet these words account for approximately 60% of everyday speech (e.g., house, water, bread, child, sleep, eat, run).
- **Norman Conquest — Battle of Hastings, October 14, 1066:** William the Conqueror (Duke of Normandy, of Danish Viking descent) defeated the Anglo-Saxon forces of Harold Godwinson, the last crowned Anglo-Saxon king of England. This conquest ushered in the Middle English period (c. 1100–1500 AD).
- **French and Latin influence on English:** For roughly 200 years after the Norman Conquest, French was the language of the English upper classes. Tens of thousands of French and Latin words flooded English, producing longer, more formal vocabulary (e.g., government, justice, parliament, liberty, courage, beauty). English absorbed rather than was replaced by these words, eventually resurging and morphing into Middle English.
- **Dual vocabulary phenomenon:** English often has two words for the same concept — a Germanic word for everyday use and a French/Latin word for formal contexts (e.g., ask/inquire, begin/commence, buy/purchase, freedom/liberty, kingly/royal).
- **Meat terminology example:** Norman aristocracy used French-derived words for prepared meats (pork, beef, mutton, poultry), while Anglo-Saxon peasants who raised the animals used the old English terms (pig, cow, sheep, chicken).
- **Closing application:** Taylor sees the hand of God in all these historical events — wars, marriages, invasions, cultural shifts — working together to shape the English language that would one day carry the King James Bible. He references Romans 8:28 as evidence that even bad human actions were used by God for good.
