## Summary
In this Sunday School lesson, Pat McCloskey walks through Psalm 51, using David's life — his remarkable faith and his devastating moral failures — as a mirror for believers today. The lesson centers on David's heartfelt cry for mercy after his sins of adultery, deception, and murder, emphasizing that true repentance means coming to God empty-handed, taking personal responsibility, and trusting solely in His grace and loving-kindness for restoration.

## Scripture
Psalm 51:1-12 — David's psalm of repentance, written in the context of his confrontation by the prophet Nathan following his sin with Bathsheba and his arrangement of Uriah's death (see also 2 Samuel 11–12).

## Key Themes
- God's mercy, loving-kindness, and tender mercies as the only basis for forgiveness
- True repentance: taking personal responsibility rather than blaming others
- Sin's power to erode moral reasoning and lead to deeper wrongdoing
- Integrity as an inward reality, not merely an outward appearance
- Restoration of joy in salvation — not loss of salvation itself
- Loving God as the motivation for holy living, not merely fear of consequences

## Notes

### David: The Man After God's Own Heart
- David displayed remarkable faith as a shepherd — killing a bear and a lion to protect his flock, and trusting God to defeat Goliath. His confidence was placed in the Lord, not in his own strength.
- David was a gifted military leader who united the twelve tribes of Israel.
- Yet David was also a poor husband, a neglectful father, and ultimately an adulterer, deceiver, and murderer in the Bathsheba-Uriah affair (2 Samuel 11–12).
- Sin caused David to lose his ability to reason clearly, leading him deeper and deeper into deception.
- David denied and concealed his sin until the prophet Nathan confronted him directly.

### Psalm 51:1 — The Cry for Mercy
- "Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness; according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies, blot out my transgressions."
- David's appeal is not based on his own merit or past good deeds — he does not point to slaying Goliath or leading Israel. His appeal rests entirely on who God is.
- He asks not for justice, but for mercy. David wanted his sin not merely forgiven but completely removed.

### Psalm 51:2 — Personal Responsibility
- "Wash me thoroughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin."
- David does not shift blame to Bathsheba, to Uriah, or to circumstances. He owns his sin fully.
- McCloskey applied this personally: we are often quick to blame others, situations, or even objects for our failures. True repentance requires looking inward.

### Psalm 51:3-4 — Confession Before God
- "For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me. Against thee, thee only, have I sinned."
- David calls his actions sin — not mistakes, not errors in judgment.
- While David sinned against Bathsheba, Uriah, and others, his primary focus is on his offense before God, who sees everything.
- God desires truth in the inward parts — sincere hearts matter more than outward religious performance.

### Psalm 51:7-12 — Request for Cleansing and Restoration
- David asks to be purged, washed, and given a clean heart and a right spirit.
- "Cast me not away from thy presence" — David feared the loss of closeness with God, not the loss of salvation itself.
- "Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation" — McCloskey noted this verse shows David had not lost his salvation, but had lost the joy of it. Eternal life remains eternal.
- McCloskey shared a personal account of waking in the night feeling distant from God, getting on his knees, confessing, and experiencing the refreshing presence of the Lord.

### Motivation for Godly Living
- The reason to live for God is not merely that sin has been forgiven, but because of love for Him and a desire to reflect His character before others.
- A daily habit of confession — coming to God each night acknowledging wrongdoing — allows believers to walk with a clean heart.
- Salvation is by grace through faith, not by works (Ephesians 2:8-9); good deeds cannot contribute to or earn salvation.
