## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues the Sunday School series on the King James Bible by giving a detailed account of John Wycliffe's life, ministry, and legacy as the "Morning Star of the Reformation." The lesson covers Wycliffe's advocacy for salvation by grace through faith, his opposition to Roman Catholic corruption and the withholding of Scripture from common people, and his foundational work in translating the Bible into Middle English. Taylor also traces the influence of Wycliffe's followers, the Lollards, and the lasting impact of Wycliffe's translation work on subsequent English Bibles, including the King James Bible.

## Notes

### Series Context
- This is Part 12 of an ongoing study on the King James Bible, examining precursors and previous translations leading up to the KJV.
- Prior lessons covered Satan's opposition to God's word, development of the English language, and earlier translation history.

### John Wycliffe's Background and Education
- Born into a well-to-do family; entered Oxford University at age 15.
- Oxford was considered one of the greatest universities in Europe at the time.
- At Oxford, Wycliffe was exposed to the writings of Thomas Bradwardine, Archbishop of Canterbury, who wrote *On the Cause of God*, arguing that the cause of God is the cause of grace — a position contrary to Roman Catholic works-based doctrine.
- This reinforced Wycliffe's inclination toward grace theology.

### Wycliffe's Advocacy and Growing Opposition
- Wycliffe became a popular and successful teacher at Oxford despite being at odds with church leadership.
- He initially opposed the corrupt practices of the Roman Catholic clergy, then increasingly challenged the doctrines underlying those practices.
- He became a strong advocate for salvation by grace through faith alone, and for making the Bible available in the common language.
- His famous statement: *"Trust wholly in Christ, rely altogether on his sufferings, beware of seeking to be justified in any other way than by his righteousness. Faith in our Lord Jesus Christ is sufficient for salvation."*
- This was considered revolutionary and dangerous by the established church.
- He was called the **Morning Star of the Reformation** because his teaching anticipated that of the later Protestant reformers.

### The Lollards
- Wycliffe's passionate student followers became known as the **Lollards** — a name meaning "babblers" (cf. Acts 17, where Paul was called a babbler by Athenian philosophers).
- A pivotal pre-Reformation Christian movement that carried on and expanded Wycliffe's teachings.
- Cornerstones of their belief: the Bible as ultimate authority in religious matters; Scripture should be accessible to all; the Bible should be translated into vernacular languages.
- They challenged the doctrine of transubstantiation, arguing that the Eucharist was symbolic rather than a literal transformation.
- They were severely persecuted — many were imprisoned or burned at the stake by both ecclesiastical and royal authorities.
- Taylor noted that those who protested from *within* the Catholic Church often paid a greater price than those, like Baptists, who were already outside it.

### Control of Knowledge
- The Roman Catholic Church maintained that scriptural interpretation belonged exclusively to the clergy — control of knowledge as an instrument of power.
- Taylor drew a parallel to modern efforts to control public education and influence the minds of the population.

### Wycliffe's Translation Work
- Wycliffe believed: *"Holy Scripture is the highest authority for every believer, the standard of faith and foundation for reform."*
- In his book *The Truth of the Holy Scriptures*, Wycliffe declared Scripture divinely inspired in every part and authoritative for all people, from peasants to kings and popes.
- Most Christians in Wycliffe's day encountered Scripture only through oral readings or homilies in Latin.
- **Early Version (EV):** First complete English Bible, a direct translation from the Latin Vulgate, produced 1380–1382 by Wycliffe and his associate Nicholas Herford. The New Testament was completed in 1380; the Old Testament in 1382 (just after Wycliffe was banned from Oxford in 1381).
- **Later Version (LV):** A greatly improved revision begun by Wycliffe and completed by his secretary and disciple John Purvey around 1388–1395, over a decade after Wycliffe's death. Purvey used the London dialect (as Chaucer used in *Canterbury Tales*), helping establish Middle English as the dominant form of the language.
- Both versions predated Gutenberg's printing press by more than half a century — every copy had to be hand-written, taking an experienced scribe approximately 10 months per Bible.
- Approximately 30 manuscripts of the Early Version and 140 manuscripts of Purvey's revision still exist.
- Wycliffe's translation work influenced the wording of the King James Bible in several places, even though it was a translation from Latin rather than from the Hebrew and Greek.

### Wycliffe's Final Years and Death
- Following a stroke in 1382, Wycliffe had to be carried into the pulpit but continued preaching and writing.
- He was summoned to Rome by the Pope to answer charges of heresy in 1383 but declined, citing the king of kings (God) as a higher authority preventing him from traveling.
- He suffered a stroke on 28 December 1384 while conducting a church service in Lutterworth and died on 31 December 1384 of natural causes.
- In 1415, the church officially branded him a heretic; in 1428 — 44 years after his death — his body was exhumed and his bones scattered.
- Nicholas Herford and John Purvey both suffered imprisonment for their translation work.
- Several of Wycliffe's friends were burned at the stake with his Bible tied around their necks.

### Legacy
- Wycliffe's translation work was the foundational "foot in the door" for the English Bible.
- It prepared the way for William Tyndale, the Geneva Bible translators, and ultimately the King James Bible of 1611.
- Taylor compared Wycliffe to the Apostle Paul: both were born into privilege, both forsook privilege for principle, and both finished their course faithfully.
- His personal character remained "unimpeachable" even to his enemies.
