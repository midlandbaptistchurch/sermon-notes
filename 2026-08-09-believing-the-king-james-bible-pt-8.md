## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues his series on the King James Bible by tracing the development of modern English, with particular focus on the Great Vowel Shift of the 15th–16th centuries and its role in maturing the English language. He connects England's growing national confidence under Queen Elizabeth I — and the rise of English as a global lingua franca — to God's providential preparation of the English language as the vehicle for the King James Bible.

## Scripture
Psalm 12:6-7 — Referenced as the foundation for the series: "The words of the Lord are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. Thou shalt keep them, O LORD, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever."

## Key Themes
- God's providential preparation of the English language for the King James Bible
- The Great Vowel Shift and the transition from Middle English to modern English
- England's rise to national confidence and linguistic maturity under Queen Elizabeth I
- English as the global lingua franca and its implications for spreading the gospel
- The authority and preservation of the King James Bible as the final authority in English

## Notes
- Taylor opens by recapping the prior lessons: Old English emerged through Anglo-Saxon and Jutish invasions; Middle English was shaped largely by the Norman (French) invasion of 1066.
- **The Great Vowel Shift** (approx. 15th–16th centuries) was a major change in the pronunciation of long vowels in English, occurring in roughly eight steps over several hundred years. It is a key reason why modern English spelling and pronunciation often do not match.
- Possible causes of the Great Vowel Shift discussed: population migration caused by the Black Plague, French linguistic influence following the Norman Conquest, social mobility and desire to distinguish class through speech, and anti-French sentiment following wars with France.
- By the late 1400s, English was no longer dismissed as the language of the lower classes but was recognized as the language of a nation with a growing sense of identity (quoting Professor Alister McGrath).
- **Queen Elizabeth I (1533–1603)** is highlighted as a pivotal figure: she developed England's military and cultural power, elevated English to the front rank of European languages through her poets, playwrights, and translators, and chose James VI of Scotland as her successor — the future King James I of England, who would authorize the King James Bible.
- George Putnam's 1589 work *Art of English Poetry* declared English as sophisticated as Greek or Latin — a sign of the language's maturity.
- McGrath is quoted: "To write in English or translate into English was a political act, affirming the intrinsic dignity of the language of a newly confident people and nation."
- **English as lingua franca**: Taylor explains the concept of a lingua franca (a common bridge language between peoples of different native tongues), illustrating it with personal anecdotes from his NATO service in Germany. He argues English now functions as the global lingua franca.
- Application: The global reach of English opens missionary doors (e.g., teaching English in China). Believers holding the King James Bible have an extraordinary opportunity to use this linguistic influence to spread the gospel.
- Taylor notes the series will continue next week with a comparison of English among world languages, and will later examine the earlier English Bible translations leading up to the King James Bible as the "seventh purification."
