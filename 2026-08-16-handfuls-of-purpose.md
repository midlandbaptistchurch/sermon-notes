## Summary
Drawing from the story of Ruth gleaning in Boaz's field, Dr. William Jeffcoat explores how what appears to be coincidence is in fact divine providence — "handfuls of purpose" left intentionally by a sovereign God. He uses Boaz as a type of Christ the kinsman-redeemer, showing that God's personal blessings are never merely for the individual recipient but are intended to flow outward as a blessing to others.

## Scripture
Ruth 2:1-10 — Ruth, a Moabite widow, arrives in Israel with her mother-in-law Naomi and seeks to glean in the fields; she "happens" upon the field of Boaz, a wealthy kinsman of her late father-in-law Elimelech, who instructs his servants to leave grain behind for her on purpose.

## Key Themes
- Divine providence over apparent coincidence
- Boaz as a type of Christ the kinsman-redeemer
- God's personal, purposeful blessings ("handfuls of purpose")
- Blessings received are meant to be passed on to others
- The universality and particularity of Christ's redemption
- Missions and the continuing purpose of God among the nations

## Notes
- **"It was her hap"** — Ruth's arrival in Boaz's field appeared accidental, but Dr. Jeffcoat emphasizes that nothing "just so happens" with God; what seems like coincidence is divine providence.
- **Boaz as type of Christ** — Boaz is described as a "mighty man of wealth" from Bethlehem who acts as kinsman-redeemer; Dr. Jeffcoat draws a direct parallel to Christ, who also came from Bethlehem, became flesh to be our kinsman-redeemer, and paid an unspeakable price that purchased more than Adam lost — "I'm headed to heaven, not Eden."
- **The Hebrew word for "handfuls"** — Dr. Jeffcoat notes this specific Hebrew word appears nowhere else in all of Scripture, underscoring the unique and intentional nature of God's provision: "There's no hand like his hand."
- **Handfuls on purpose** — Boaz instructed his servants to deliberately leave extra grain for Ruth. Dr. Jeffcoat applies this to Scripture passages that seem written just for us, and to providential circumstances in our lives — personal illustrations include God's provision during 29 years of mission work in India.
- **The ephah of barley** — Ruth left the field with an ephah (10 times an omer). This abundance points to the truth that God's blessing on an individual is never only for that individual; it is always meant to overflow to others.
- **Joseph and Egypt** — Dr. Jeffcoat connects the story to Joseph opening the storehouses of Egypt, with nations drawn into his presence, as a picture of God's continuing purpose to draw sinners to Himself through the blessings He pours out.
- **The time of the judges** — The story takes place when "every man did that which was right in his own eyes," yet God's redemptive purpose was not hindered. Application: however dark the present age, God will continue to draw precious sinners to Himself.
- **Altar call** — Dr. Jeffcoat closed with a personal plea for anyone unsure of their salvation, reminding the congregation that Christ would have died for any one person alone, and that every blessing received carries the responsibility to be a blessing to others.
