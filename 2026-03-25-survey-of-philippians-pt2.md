## Summary
Pastor Jim continues the survey of Philippians, explaining that this epistle addresses the problem of disunity in the church at Philippi. Written 10 years after the church's founding, Paul reproves the believers for failing to maintain the unity of spirit and one mind that should characterize the body of Christ. The core theme is pursuing God's high calling through lowliness of mind and servant-heartedness—the pattern modeled by Christ himself.

## Scripture
Philippians 1-4 — Paul's prison epistle to the church at Philippi, addressing unity, joy, and Christian conduct.

## Key Themes
- Unity and oneness in the body of Christ
- Lowliness of mind and servant-heartedness as the path to joy
- The contrast between the Acts period (emphasis on the Jew first) and the prison epistles (emphasis on the body of Christ)
- Reproof for disunity: the dispute between Euodia and Syntyche
- Pressing toward the high calling of God
- Maintaining joy through proper perspective and fellowship

## Notes

### Context and Structure
- Philippians is the shortest of Paul's epistles: 4 chapters, 104 verses
- The church at Philippi was founded during Paul's second missionary journey (Acts 16) and was one of the first churches in Europe
- By the time Paul writes (62 AD), the church has grown significantly and now has multiple bishops and deacons
- This letter follows the pattern: doctrine (Ephesians), reproof (Philippians), correction (Colossians)

### Paul's Prison Epistles vs. Acts Period Writings
- During Acts, Paul emphasizes reaching "the Jew first" (Romans 3:1, Acts 13:46, 18:6, 28:28)
- After Acts 28, when Paul is imprisoned, his epistles (Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians) reveal a higher understanding of God's purpose for the body of Christ
- The mystery of the body of Christ—Jews and Gentiles united into one body—is unique to Paul's revelation
- In Philippians 3, Paul shows how his former Jewish credentials (circumcision, tribe of Benjamin, Pharisaic righteousness) he now counts as loss for Christ

### The Problem at Philippi: Disunity
- The church faces a critical problem: believers are not maintaining unity of spirit and mind
- Specific example: Euodia and Syntyche, two women in the church, are in open conflict (Philippians 4:2-3)
- This dispute is significant enough that Paul, while imprisoned, hears about it and addresses it by name
- The problem stems from individual opinions, pride, and failure to remember their identity in Christ

### The Solution: One Mind and One Spirit
- Philippians 1:27 and 2:2-5 emphasize "one mind," "same love," and "one accord"
- Believers must avoid murmuring and disputing (2:14-15)
- The remedy is found in remembering the "consolation of Christ"—reminding one another of who we are in Christ and what He has done
- Fellowship, mercy, compassion, and humility must characterize relationships in the local church

### The Pattern: Christ as Servant
- Christ is the ultimate example of servant-heartedness
- He took on the form of a servant and humbled Himself to death on the cross (Philippians 2:7-8)
- True servanthood means accepting service without complaint or need for recognition
- A genuine servant is one who, when treated as a servant, takes it well

### The High Calling Through Lowliness
- The paradox of Philippians: pursue God's high calling by living in lowliness of mind
- Paul himself, though spiritually mature, presses toward the mark of the high calling (3:12-14)
- Joy is found not in pursuing recognition but in service with humility
- This is why Philippians is often called "the joy book"—true joy comes through proper perspective and servant-heartedness

### Practical Application
- Growing churches face increasing challenges to unity as membership increases
- Church organization and administration are necessary but should not overshadow the primary purpose: opening Scripture and learning about God
- Believers must daily "forget those things which are behind" (3:13) and press forward
- The antidote to murmuring and complaining is engaging in witness and testimony to the world
