## Summary
This lesson introduces William Tyndale and his foundational work in English Bible translation, positioning him as producing the first Bible translated from original Greek and Hebrew texts into modern English. The session establishes three key precursor events that enabled Tyndale's work: the invention of the Gutenberg printing press (c. 1450), its introduction to England by William Caxton (1476), and the Protestant Reformation. The teacher emphasizes the value of direct access to Scripture in one's native language and challenges the class to consider their responsibility to share biblical truth with others, drawing parallels between historical martyrs and contemporary believers.

## Scripture
Psalm 12:6-7 — "The words of the Lord are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. Thou shalt keep them, O Lord, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever." This passage serves as the theme for understanding the King James Bible as the seventh purification from the Wycliffe Bible in English translations.

## Key Themes
- **Preservation of God's Word**: God has preserved His pure words through a purification process represented by seven English translations culminating in the King James Bible
- **Direct Access to Scripture**: The importance of believers having God's Word in their native language without intermediaries controlling access to truth
- **Sacrifice for Truth**: Historical translators like William Tyndale literally gave their lives to provide Scripture in English
- **Personal Responsibility**: Each believer must study God's Word individually rather than depending solely on church leaders
- **Sharing the Gospel**: The challenge to contemporary believers to demonstrate the same passion for spreading biblical truth that historical martyrs showed

## Notes

### Historical Context: Wycliffe Bible
- John Wycliffe produced the first complete English translation in the 14th century (1380-1397)
- Translated from the Latin Vulgate into Middle English
- Approximately 180 manuscripts survive
- Written in Middle English, making it practically foreign to modern English readers
- Though technically an English version, not particularly practical for contemporary application

### Three Key Precursor Events

#### 1. The Gutenberg Printing Press (c. 1450)
- Johannes Gutenberg invented the printing press, though not the first to do so
- Chinese developed wooden block printing around 800-900 AD
- Koreans used metal type about 500-600 years before Gutenberg
- Chinese also experimented with hardened clay characters
- Gutenberg's innovation: used metal type with specially developed ink containing soot
- Received funding from a man named Faust (800 guilders initially, another 800 later)
- First printing: the Gutenberg Bible in Latin
- Faust eventually won a court case against Gutenberg and took control of the press
- Before printing presses, all books were hand-copied by manuscript writers
- Books were expensive and somewhat of a status symbol
- Only more well-to-do people could typically afford books

#### 2. Printing Press Comes to England (1476)
- William Caxton brought the printing press to England
- King Edward VI and his queen visited Caxton's shop to see demonstrations
- Initially, England did not welcome printing of Bibles
- English Bibles had to be printed in Europe and brought into England

#### 3. The Protestant Reformation
- People recognized problems with the Roman Catholic Church
- Reformers saw abuses and disadvantages the common people faced
- Church system manipulated people and denied them access to truth
- The Reformation showed "it doesn't have to be that way"
- Anabaptists represented a strain of believers separate from Roman Catholicism
- Interestingly, major Bible translation developments came from people within established religions who became disgruntled with fallacies they observed

### William Tyndale's Significance
- Called "the Apostle of England" by John Fox in Fox's Book of Martyrs
- First to translate portions of the Bible from Greek and Hebrew texts into modern English (not from Latin)
- Went beyond the Latin Vulgate directly to original language texts
- One less translation step to get to the truth
- Completed the New Testament and portions of the Old Testament
- Did not complete the entire Bible in his lifetime
- Had to publish his work in Germany due to English opposition
- Considered a heretic in England for wanting to translate the Bible into English
- Burned at the stake in 1536
- Fox's Book of Martyrs includes a chapter titled "The Life and Story of the True Servant and Martyr of God, William Tyndale"

### Life Phases of William Tyndale (Introduction)
- Two main phases identified: homeland phase and later work
- Homeland phase: still in England, where God developed him
- Developed his own love for the Word of God and dedication to it
- Learned to disdain the leadership of the established church
- Realized church leaders manipulated people by keeping them from direct access to truth
- Recognized everyone needed Scripture in their own language

### Cultural Context Before Modern Entertainment
- People in the 1500s had no internet, television, or radio
- Most entertainment and education came through conversation
- People sat together and talked, sharing oral histories
- Stories were passed along verbally
- Books were rare and precious before printing presses
- Personal example: family gatherings under oak trees, sharing stories for hours
- Such interactions were grounding and developmental
- Conversation built relationships and bonded communities
