## Summary
Pastor Jim Payne continues a survey of the book of Colossians, reviewing the historical background of the church at Colossae and Paul's unique apostolic calling to the Gentiles. He walks through Colossians 1:1–5, examining Paul's apostleship by the will of God, the founding of the Colossian church through Epaphras, and the distinguishing triad of faith, love, and hope that marks the Christian life in this dispensation of grace.

## Scripture
Colossians 1:1–5 — Paul's salutation and thanksgiving to the saints at Colossae, highlighting his apostolic authority, the church's faith in Christ Jesus, their love for the saints, and the hope laid up for believers in heaven.

## Key Themes
- Paul's apostleship by the will of God and his unique calling as apostle to the Gentiles
- Rightly dividing Paul's Acts epistles from his Prison epistles
- The founding of the church at Colossae through Epaphras during Paul's Ephesian ministry
- The dispensation of grace — God's current message to the world
- The Christian triad: faith toward Christ, love toward the brethren, hope laid up in heaven
- The body of Christ as a truth revealed exclusively through the apostle Paul
- Israel's temporary setting aside and the distinct heavenly hope of the church

## Notes

### Review of Colossians Background
- Colossians has 4 chapters, 95 verses, and 1,979 words; it is one of Paul's Prison Epistles written from Rome.
- Paul had never personally visited Colossae (cf. Colossians 2:1 — "as many as have not seen my face in the flesh").
- Colossians and Ephesians are described as "twin sisters" — closely related but with distinct emphases.

### Founding of the Colossian Church
- During Paul's nearly three-year ministry in Ephesus (Acts 19:10), the Gospel spread throughout Asia.
- A man named Epaphras, from Colossae, came to Ephesus, was saved, returned home, and planted a church — likely meeting in the home of Philemon.
- Philemon's son Archippus eventually became pastor of the church at Colossae.
- While Paul was imprisoned in Rome, Epaphras visited him, was himself imprisoned, and gave Paul a report on the Colossian church — prompting the writing of this letter.

### Paul's Two Ministries: Acts vs. Prison
- Paul's ministry is divided into his "Acts ministry" (going to the Jew first, accommodating Jewish practices) and his "Prison ministry" (the full revelation of the mystery of Christ and the body of Christ).
- In the Prison Epistles, Paul counts his former accommodations as loss and focuses entirely on the excellency of the knowledge of Christ (Philippians 3).
- The body of Christ reaches its fullest understanding during Paul's prison ministry and release period (1 Timothy).

### Colossians 1:1 — Paul's Apostleship
- Paul always wrote his name in his own hand as the authenticating token of every epistle (2 Thessalonians 3:17).
- He was not one of the Twelve — that required following Christ in His earthly ministry — but he was a true apostle, having seen the risen Christ (1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8).
- Paul describes himself as "one born out of due time" — Gentile salvation according to prophecy comes through a risen Israel, but Paul was raised up to bring Gentiles into the body of Christ outside that prophetic order, by grace.
- His apostleship was "by the will of God" — part of God's plan to reconcile heaven and earth under the authority of Christ.

### The Body of Christ
- The body of Christ is a truth found only in Romans through Philemon; no Old Testament writer or even Christ in the Gospels revealed it.
- It consists of Jews and Gentiles, male and female, bond and free — one new man in Christ.
- Members of the body are destined to be seated with Christ in heavenly places, inheriting thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers.

### Colossians 1:2 — Saints and Faithful Brethren
- Addressed to those both "in Christ" (salvation) and "at Colossae" (local church assembly).
- Believers need to be in Christ for salvation and in a local church for sanctification and growth.
- Paul specifically addresses the "faithful" — those who can be trusted as stewards of the truth of the mystery (cf. 2 Timothy 2:2).

### Colossians 1:3 — Grace and Peace
- The salutation "grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" is not merely a greeting but a message from heaven.
- The Holy Spirit is not mentioned because He is already present, dwelling within believers.
- Today is still the day of grace; one day it will give way to the day of Christ, the day of the Lord, and ultimately the day of God.

### Colossians 1:4–5 — Faith, Love, and Hope
- Paul commends the Colossians for their faith in Christ Jesus, their love toward all the saints, and their hope laid up in heaven.
- "Christ Jesus" (rather than "Jesus Christ") is Paul's Prison Epistle designation, emphasizing Christ in His ascended, glorified position far above all principalities and powers.
- **Faith** is active — it is demonstrated by works (cf. James; 1 Thessalonians 1:3 — "work of faith").
- **Love** abounds in knowledge and discernment (Philippians 1:9); it requires effort — a "labor of love."
- **Hope** is laid up in heaven — a distinctly Pauline and body-of-Christ hope. Israel's hope was earthly (the land, the kingdom); the church's hope is heavenly (caught up to be with Christ, then reigning in heavenly places).
- This triad also appears in 1 Thessalonians 1:3: work of faith, labor of love, patience of hope.

### Israel and the Gentiles
- Romans 15:8 — Christ came as a minister to the circumcision to confirm the promises to the fathers.
- Christ's earthly ministry was directed to Israel (Matthew 10:5–6; 15:24).
- Israel rejected the Father (Gospels), the Son (Gospels), and the Holy Spirit (Acts 7 — stoning of Stephen).
- God set Israel aside and, rather than bringing judgment, saved Saul of Tarsus — the chief opponent — inaugurating the dispensation of grace.
- Isaiah 60:1–3 and Romans 15:9–12 describe the future time when Israel rises and Gentile nations flow to Israel's Messiah; today Gentiles are saved in spite of Israel's blindness, not through it.

### Closing Prayer Themes
- Colossians is a book of correction — correcting believers regarding the Gospel and the body of Christ.
- The pre-tribulational hope of the catching up of the saints is a present encouragement to all believers.
