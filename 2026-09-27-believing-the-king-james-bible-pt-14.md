## Summary
Hugh Taylor continues the ongoing Sunday School series on the King James Bible by examining the production history of the Textus Receptus (TR), the Greek text underlying the KJV. The lesson focuses primarily on the life and character of Desiderius Erasmus — his background, education, humanist philosophy, and his pivotal role in compiling and publishing the first printed Greek New Testament. Taylor also explores the historical divide between those who championed the Latin Vulgate and those who asserted the superiority and providential preservation of the Greek New Testament text.

### Overview
- This is Part 14 of an extended study on the King James Bible, continuing a look at the production history of the Textus Receptus (TR).
- The series is working toward examining the pre-KJV English Bible versions and what Taylor calls the "seven steps of purification" leading to the KJV.

### Key Figures in Producing the Greek Text (TR)
Taylor introduces the main compilers of the Greek text:
- **Desiderius Erasmus** (b. 1466 or 1469) — the primary and most celebrated editor
- **Robert Etienne** (Robertus Stefanus / Robert Stephens) — French scholar, 1519–1605
- **Elzevir** — a printer and his relatives who also contributed to the TR tradition

### Human Influence and Collaborative Process
- Taylor notes that any human involvement in God's work brings individual biases and flaws, but that multiple contributors can offset each other's weaknesses and enhance each other's strengths.
- The printing industry served as a commercial engine that God used to drive the production and dissemination of Scripture, even when publishers had commercial motives.

### Desiderius Erasmus: Life and Background
- Born in Rotterdam, an illegitimate child of a Catholic priest (Gerard) and Margaret Rutgers.
- Raised in a loving household until both parents died of bubonic plague in 1483, when Erasmus was a teenager.
- Educated in Deventer at a school notable for being the first in northern Europe (below university level) to teach Greek — where Erasmus first learned Greek.
- Pressured by his uncle/guardian into monastic life at Stein, South Holland, for financial reasons rather than spiritual calling.
- Ordained a priest in 1492 (the same year Columbus sailed) but never functioned as one in any meaningful way.
- Later received a papal dispensation overcoming the canonical barrier of his illegitimate birth.
- In his work *De Contemptu Mundi* (written 1490s, published 1521), he criticized monastic life and expressed regret at having been coerced into it.

### Erasmus as Scholar and Traveler
- Erasmus worked as a tutor to wealthy students in Paris rather than accepting a staff position, preserving his personal freedom.
- Traveled extensively through England (including five years teaching at Cambridge, 1509–1514), France, the Netherlands, Italy, Germany, and Basel, Switzerland.
- Basel was the hub of his greatest legacy: he published the first printed Greek New Testament there in 1516 and died there in 1536.
- Sought after by five European heads of government for professorships and court positions; generally avoided being tied down.
- Known as a "humanist" in the Renaissance sense — believing humanity could be improved through faith and education, not in the modern secular humanist sense.
- Famous quote: *"The main hope of a nation lies in the proper education of its youth."*

### Erasmus' Theological Stance and Mission
- Though rooted in Roman Catholicism, Erasmus spoke out against the Church's abuses and rejected the authority of the Latin Vulgate as the sole New Testament text.
- He had an intense burden to place God's Word in the hands of ordinary people in their own languages.
- Famous quote: *"I would have those words translated into all languages so that not only Scots and Irishmen, but Turks and Saracens might read them. I long for the plow boy to sing them to himself as he follows the plow, the weaver to hum them to the tune of his shuttle, the traveler to beguile with them the dullness of his journey."*
- He believed the biblical text should determine doctrine, not the other way around — contrasted with paraphrases like Kenneth Taylor's *Living Bible*, which Taylor argues reflected the translator's own doctrinal bias.

### The Great Divide: Latin Vulgate vs. Greek Text
- Two camps existed in Erasmus' day:
  1. **Pro-Latin Vulgate**: Rooted in loyalty to Roman Catholic authority; the Vulgate was the only recognized NT text.
  2. **Pro-Greek Text**: Held that Scripture had been providentially preserved in the Greek New Testament, with the Latin being secondary and supplementary.
- Erasmus, his circle of reformers, and even Pope Leo X took the side of the Greek text's superiority.
- Erasmus dedicated his first edition of the Greek New Testament to Pope Leo X.
- This pro-Greek position was held more as a matter of faith than a fully articulated scholarly theory at the time.
- Taylor affirms this position: God preserved His Word primarily in the Greek text, in the majority of manuscripts, despite Satan's ongoing efforts to corrupt and undermine it.

### Application and Closing Thoughts
- Taylor draws a recurring parallel: just as God used flawed individuals (David, Paul, Erasmus) to accomplish His purposes, He can use every believer's strengths despite their weaknesses.
- The "grit" required of missionaries is compared to the determination of figures like Erasmus — both can be simultaneously effective and abrasive.
- Taylor notes he previously used multiple Bible translations to find wording that suited his pre-formed ideas, but now lets the KJV text determine his thinking.
- Next week's lesson will look more closely at Erasmus' specific views on text preservation and how he came to champion the Greek New Testament.
