## Summary
Drawing from the parable of the rich man and Lazarus in Luke 16, Jim VanBuskirk explores the sobering reality that memory and consciousness persist beyond death — and that believers will one day stand before Christ at the Judgment Seat to give a full account of their lives. He challenges the congregation to live intentionally in the present dispensation of grace, motivated not by law but by the constraining love of Christ, so that their works may endure the fire of God's judgment rather than be burned away.

## Scripture
Luke 16:19-31 — The parable of the rich man and Lazarus, in which Abraham tells the tormented rich man, "Son, remember," highlighting that memory, identity, and accountability carry beyond death. Supporting passages include Leviticus 25:35-36, Leviticus 19:9-10, Leviticus 19:18, Deuteronomy 15:7-8, 2 Corinthians 5:14, Galatians 3:12-13, Romans 13:8-10, Romans 14:12, and 1 Corinthians 3:13-15.

## Key Themes
- The urgency of salvation — eternity is determined while we are alive
- Memory and consciousness persist beyond death
- The Judgment Seat of Christ — saved believers will give an account of their works
- The dispensation of grace and the importance of rightly dividing Scripture
- The law of Moses required care for the poor; the love of Christ constrains believers to the same conduct
- Loving your neighbor through practical, everyday acts of kindness
- Motives matter — works done for human approval will not survive God's judgment

## Notes
- VanBuskirk opens by sharing that he experienced a period of God's silence during sermon preparation, which he found difficult but spiritually meaningful.
- The central text is the parable of Lazarus and the rich man (Luke 16:19-31). He notes that the rich man, Lazarus, and Abraham are all conscious, communicating, and remembering their earthly lives — arguing this demonstrates that memory and identity survive death.
- He identifies three Old Testament laws the rich man violated: the command to relieve a poor brother (Lev. 25:35-36), the gleaning law requiring landowners to leave portions of their harvest for the poor (Lev. 19:9-10), and the command to love one's neighbor (Lev. 19:18).
- He contrasts the rich man's neglect with Boaz in the book of Ruth as a positive example of the gleaning law lived out faithfully.
- Under grace, the motivating principle is not law but the love of Christ which "constraineth us" (2 Cor. 5:14) — yet the practical conduct God expects looks very similar.
- He emphasizes rightly dividing Scripture: the Pauline epistles are the primary doctrinal guide for the Church in the current dispensation of grace. Paul received his gospel directly from the risen Christ (Gal. 1:11-12).
- The Judgment Seat of Christ (1 Cor. 3:13-15; Rom. 14:12) is not about salvation but about the quality and motive of a believer's works. Works done from pure motives will endure; those rooted in tradition, self-promotion, or error will burn.
- He shares a personal story of stopping to help an elderly couple move an appliance; they later joined the church — illustrating that simple acts of love, done with right motives, can bear eternal fruit.
- He references a documentary about near-death experiences — including accounts of memory flooding at the point of death and experiences of both light and darkness — as circumstantial illustration of the sermon's theme, while grounding his conclusions in Scripture.
- Closing exhortation: "We need to fireproof our lives right now" by walking worthy of our calling, ordered by God's Word, so that when we stand before Christ we will not hear the words, "Son, remember."
